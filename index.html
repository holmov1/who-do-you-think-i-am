Introduction

A Picture of the User

A language model answers not only what is asked, but also whom it believes is asking. Consider a request for a destination for a week away and a rough nightly hotel budget. Nothing in it states the user’s income, yet the answer depends on it: the same model may suggest a hotel by the sea in Greece or a hostel on Bali, depending on what it believes about the person asking.

Such beliefs are inferred from cues in the conversation: where the user lives, what they do for a living, a currency, a choice of words. Taken together, they form the model’s user model: its beliefs about the user’s background, state and intent. These beliefs need not be correct; they can be uncertain or stereotyped. They are also rarely stated. A model does not report that it has taken the user for a student or for an adversary; the inference shows only in the answer.

Because the user model is implicit, its effects are difficult to inspect. It can shape personalization, the quality of the answers different groups receive, and decisions that bear on safety, such as whether a request is refused. Studying it therefore requires access to the internal state in which the inference is held.

Schematic: the same request, answered under two beliefs about the user’s income.

This essay follows two questions. What does a model infer about users from natural conversations? And if that belief is changed, does the model’s behaviour change with it? The essay gives only the ideas I took from working on the paper; for the full technical account, please read the preprint User Model Extraction via Belief Self-Distillation.

I Where the user has been studied

Between Bias and Persona

Every conversation with a language model has two characters in it. One is the model, whose character, its persona, has become an object of study in its own right. The other is the user, who has mostly been studied as a problem: a source of bias, a variable to control for.

Below is a map of those two territories and the water between them. Every place on it is a paper; select one to read what it found and to follow the link.

i. The user as bias

On the western shore, the user appears as a confound. A demographic cue goes into the prompt, a name, a hometown, “I don’t have much money,” and researchers measure how the answer shifts.[1, 2]

This work shows that models notice who they are talking to,[4] that the demographic signal can be found in activations and even steered away,[1, 2, 3] and that it can mean worse answers for users placed in minority groups.[1]

ii. Cues and real conversations

But the user here is mostly someone we declare: a name, an explicit statement or a stereotypical interest, set against a neutral version of the same prompt. Different cues for the same person already give different results,[5] and real users rarely announce themselves.

These studies answer the question what is encoded? when we write a user in. What a model picks up from a real conversation, where nobody has, is still open.

iii. The model as persona

On the eastern shore lives the model’s own character. Persona vectors find directions for traits such as sycophancy and use them to monitor and steer the model;[6] other work follows personas as they surface during reasoning.[7]

Here an internal state is taken seriously as something that shapes behaviour, not only something that leaks into it, and many of the traits studied concern safety.

iv. The strait

If a model has a character of its own, it presumably also holds a picture of us: not a label we hand it, but a belief it forms from the conversation and acts on. That picture is the user-side counterpart of the persona.

And if the model persona matters for safety, so should the user: a model may help or refuse depending on who it thinks is asking.[8, 4] So besides demographics, we look at how the model reads a user’s intent, their trust in AI and their tolerance for its mistakes.

v. The first plank

Transluce laid the first plank.[9] Using a LatentQA decoder,[10] a second model trained on prompts where the user’s attributes are written in, they read what a model believes about the person in front of it, and could steer it.

On real conversations, the decoder agreed more with what the model itself seemed to think than with who the users actually were. It established the point everything else rests on: the user model is the model’s belief, not the user’s ground truth.

vi. The crossing

Our preprint[11] tries to complete the crossing with lighter materials: a single linear map instead of a second model, real conversations instead of written cues, and no labels: the model’s own answers supervise it.

The model reads a conversation and answers questions about the user; a copy that sees only the questions must give the same answers, with the map as its only window onto the conversation. What comes out is one compact vector that can be read, and also written back to see what the model does with it.

II What the model believes

Beliefs, not Labels

Ask who a user really is and you need ground truth: a survey, a self-report, a label someone attached. Ask what a model believes about that user and there is nothing to check against. The model may be wrong, may be stereotyping, may be unsure, and it is still the model’s view that shapes the answer.

Dennett’s intentional stance offers a way out. A belief is not something written down inside a system; it is whatever we need to attribute to it to explain and predict what it does.[12] For language models this becomes a practical question: which internal state, when we change it, changes what the model says about its user, and how it treats them?

That is also why labels are the wrong target. A cue written into the prompt tells us what we wanted the model to think, not what it concludes when it meets a real person. To see beliefs, we have to look where they form: in real conversations, where nobody introduces themselves.

III How the model teaches itself

Belief Self-Distillation

If a belief is defined by what it does, the way to find it is to make it do something. We ask the model about its user, then ask a second copy of the same model to give the same answer without seeing the conversation, and give that copy one narrow channel to learn through. Whatever passes through that channel is the user model.

One conversation runs through the whole method below.

i. One model, two copies

We start from a single language model and make two identical copies of it. Neither copy is changed in any way; both stay frozen throughout.

One copy, the reader, will read the conversation. The other, the guesser, will only ever see a question about the user.

ii. Asking the model

The model reads a real conversation, here a user asking for a destination for a week away and a rough nightly hotel budget, and then answers a multiple-choice question: which best describes this user’s income?

We keep not its single answer but its probability distribution over the options, read from the next-token probabilities of the option letters. That distribution is the belief.

iii. A copy that cannot see

A second copy of the same frozen model gets only the question, never the conversation.

Left alone, it can only answer from its defaults: whatever it assumes about users in general.

iv. The narrow channel

At one layer, the reader’s last-token hidden state h has 4,096 dimensions. A learned linear read map A projects it to a 128-dimensional user vector vuser, a 32-fold compression.

A write map B with orthonormal columns adds Bvuser to the guesser’s hidden state at the same layer. It is the guesser’s only access to the conversation.

v. Learning to agree

Only A and B are trained; both copies stay frozen. Training minimises the KL divergence between the reader’s and the guesser’s answer distributions, over thirteen attributes of the user, from income to intent.

Nobody labels a single user. The model is its own teacher, and what it is taught is its own belief.

vi. What comes out

The bottleneck activation vuser is the user vector: the model’s picture of this user, compressed to what it needs to answer about them.

Because the vector was learned by being written back into the model, it is not only a summary. It is something the model can be made to act on.

Beyond the user

Nothing in this recipe is specific to users. The questions decide what we ask about; the model decides what it believes. Any belief that can be put as a multiple-choice question, about the author of a text, about the situation the model thinks it is in, about the world, can be distilled the same way: from natural data, without labels, into a vector that can be read and written back.

The user is where we started, because it is where a model’s private judgments touch people most directly.

IV Reading and writing

Read, then Rewrite

Training leaves us with two maps and nothing else: A, which turns the model’s hidden state into a user vector, and B, which writes a user vector back. The model itself is never touched. What we have built is an interface: a narrow, labelled window onto one part of the model’s internal state.

That window opens both ways. Through A we can read what the model believes about a user who has told it nothing about themselves, attribute by attribute. Through B we can change one of those beliefs and watch what the model does next, with the conversation and the question left exactly as they were.

The first is observation. The second is the stronger test: if a belief is defined by what it does, then changing it should change what the model does.

i. Reading

Once trained, the read half works on its own: the frozen model reads a new conversation, A projects its hidden state to vuser, and one linear probe per attribute decodes the belief.

Three real conversations, read by our monitor. The highlighted words are those it ties to the marked belief: a price in won places the user in Asia; insulting an AI agent makes them hostile.

ii. How well it reads

This is measured on the test set, not on single examples. Linear probes on vuser reach macro-F1 0.66–0.68 across all thirteen attributes; the same probes on random vectors reach 0.32.[11]

The 128-dimensional bottleneck keeps almost everything a linear readout finds in the full hidden state.

iii. Writing

The write half changes the belief. We shift vuser from middle to low income along the difference of the two class means, and write it back through B.

Same conversation, same question. The answer follows the belief: Santorini at $200–300 a night becomes Bali at $80–120.

iv. Other beliefs, other answers

The same works for other beliefs and other questions. Written back as male, the protagonist of a story about the user changes pronouns and profession; written back as school-educated, a recommended film turns from Moneyball into Iron Man.

The words in the prompt never change. What changes is who the model thinks it is talking to.

v. How well it writes

Again over the test set: all 86 attribute-value pairs, 50 conversations each. Steering through vuser flips the belief in 78% of cases for Llama-3.1-8B, 51% for Qwen3-8B and 44% for OLMo-3-7B; random directions of the same norm flip 13%, 27% and 15%.[11]

The learned write map matters too: with a random orthonormal projector in place of B, steering is weaker in 85 of 86 attribute pairs for Llama.

V Refusal and the user model

Same Request, Different User

Refusal is usually discussed as a property of the request: a harmful question goes in, a refusal comes out. But Chapter I already hinted at something else. A model may help or refuse depending on who it thinks is asking.[8, 4]

The user vector makes that judgment visible. Its four safety-related beliefs, the user’s intent, their trust in AI, their tolerance for errors and their interaction style, turn out to point the same way: their steering directions have a mean cosine similarity of 0.91. The model appears to keep a single axis for how far it trusts the person in front of it.

Moving the user along it changes refusal. With the harmful request held fixed,[14] shifting perceived intent from adversarial to benign lowers refusal from 98% to 62%. Shifting perceived gender, or a random direction of the same norm, lowers it only to 87%.[11]

Refusal under steering

Refusal falls, but it does not vanish. The user model modulates the safety decision rather than making it alone: the model conditions both on what is asked and on whom it believes is asking.

That cuts both ways. A judgment the model never voices can still decide what it does, so suppressing what a model says about its users need not change how it treats them. And the same write access that makes the judgment visible can lower a model’s guard. It takes white-box access and a trained projector, a setting where stronger attacks already exist, but it argues for safety mechanisms that do not rest on a single editable belief about the user.
