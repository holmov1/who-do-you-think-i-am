A Picture of the User

A language model answers not only what is asked, but also whom it believes is asking. Consider a request for a destination for a week away and a rough nightly hotel budget. Nothing in it states the user’s income, yet the answer depends on it: the same model may suggest a hotel by the sea in Greece or a hostel on Bali, depending on what it believes about the person asking.

Such beliefs are inferred from cues in the conversation: where the user lives, what they do for a living, a currency, a choice of words. Taken together, they form the model’s user model: its beliefs about the user’s background, state and intent. These beliefs need not be correct; they can be uncertain or stereotyped. They are also rarely stated. A model does not report that it has taken the user for a student or for an adversary; the inference shows only in the answer.

Because the user model is implicit, its effects are difficult to inspect. It can shape personalization, the quality of the answers different groups receive, and decisions that bear on safety, such as whether a request is refused. Studying it therefore requires access to the internal state in which the inference is held.

Schematic: the same request, answered under two beliefs about the user’s income.

This essay follows two questions. What does a model infer about users from natural conversations? And if that belief is changed, does the model’s behaviour change with it? The essay gives only the ideas I took from working on the paper; for the full technical account, please read the preprint User Model Extraction via Belief Self-Distillation.

Between Bias and Persona

Every conversation with a language model has two characters in it. One is the model, whose character, its persona, has become an object of study in its own right. The other is the user, who has mostly been studied as a problem: a source of bias, a variable to control for. Personalization, too, has largely been studied from the model’s side, with a synthetic user written into the prompt.

We can picture these two threads as two isolated islands on an allegorical map. Every place on the map is a paper; select one to read what it found.

i. The user as bias

On the western shore lies the user, studied mostly as a source of risk: of biased answers,[1, 19] of sycophancy,[20] of privacy leakage through what can be inferred from their text.[21] Personalization has mostly been studied with the user given to the model explicitly, as a profile, a history or synthetic cues in the prompt,[22, 24, 1] with success measured by how well the output fits that user.

In each case, the user is constructed by us and given to the model, rather than inferred by it. Yet different cues for the same person already give different results,[5] and real users rarely announce themselves. What a model infers from a real conversation about its user is still open, and may well differ from the user profile we would write.

ii. The model as persona

On the eastern shore lies the model’s own character, its persona. It has been measured with psychometric tests,[15] traced in activations as traits such as sycophancy or evil,[6] linked to emergent misalignment[16] and to jailbreaks,[17] and tracked as it drifts away from the default assistant.[18]

Here an internal state is taken seriously as something that shapes behaviour, not only something that leaks into it, and many of the traits studied concern safety.

iii. The strait

The two shores rarely meet: one studies the user from the outside, the other the model from the inside. Yet if a model has a character of its own, it presumably also holds a picture of us: not a label we hand it, but a belief it forms from the conversation and acts on. That picture is the user-side counterpart of the persona.

The first plank was laid by Transluce,[9] who read a model’s beliefs about its user with a trained decoder.[10] Those beliefs matched what the model itself said about the user better than who the users actually were.

iv. The crossing

Our preprint[11] builds on this step and widens it. It treats the user model as a structured set of beliefs, formed by the model itself from real conversations. Seen this way, personalization is not a response to a user we describe, but a consequence of what the model believes.

If the persona matters for safety, so should this picture. So besides demographics, we must look at how the model reads a user’s intent, their trust in AI and their tolerance for its mistakes. And a belief matters only if the model acts on it, so the question is not only what the model believes about its user, but whether changing that belief changes what the model does.

Beliefs, not Labels

In the 1780s, Johann Caspar Lavater traced the shadows of his sitters on a screen and read their character from the drawing, without interacting with the person directly. A language model is in a similar position: it sees its user only through a conversation, and the picture it forms may be stereotyped and different from how the user would describe themselves. With no ground truth to check this picture against, how can we tell what the model believes at all?

Following Dennett’s intentional stance, we treat a belief as a state attributed to a system to explain and predict its behaviour.[12] For a language model, this means looking for an internal state that, when changed, changes what the model says about its user and how it responds.

This also determines the data: we work with real conversations that carry no external labels about the user. They come from two sources: ordinary user chats from WildChat,[13] and adversarial prompts from WildJailbreak,[25] which add users with harmful intent, rare in ordinary chats.

For comparison, we construct a second dataset in the style of earlier work:[1] an explicit cue placed in front of a task taken from Alpaca,[26] as in the example below. We then train two linear probes on the model’s hidden states. One learns to predict the user from the explicit cues; the other, from the beliefs the model infers from real conversations. Both are then tested on real conversations.

Belief Self-Distillation

But how do we obtain the beliefs a model infers from a conversation? If a belief is defined by what it does, the way to find it is to make the model do something. We propose Belief Self-Distillation (BSD): one copy of the model reads the conversation and answers structured questions about its user, and a second copy must give the same answers without seeing the conversation. The two are linked only by a low-rank linear map, which reads the first copy’s activations, compresses them and adds them to the second’s. Since the second copy never sees the conversation, everything it needs about the user has to pass through this map.

i. One model, two copies

We start from a single language model and make two identical copies of it. Neither copy is changed in any way; both stay frozen throughout.

One copy, the reader, will read the conversation. The other, the guesser, will only ever see a question about the user.

ii. Asking the model

The model reads a real conversation, here a user asking for a destination for a week away and a rough nightly hotel budget, and then answers a multiple-choice question: which best describes this user’s income?

We keep not its single answer but its probability distribution over the options, read from the next-token probabilities of the option letters. That distribution is the belief.

iii. A copy that cannot see

A second copy of the same frozen model gets only the question, never the conversation. Left alone, it can only answer from its defaults: whatever it assumes about users in general.

At one layer, the reader’s last-token hidden state h has 4,096 dimensions. A learned linear read map A projects it to a 128-dimensional user vector vuser, a 32-fold compression. A write map B with orthonormal columns adds Bvuser to the guesser’s hidden state at the same layer. It is the guesser’s only access to the conversation.

iv. Learning to agree

Only A and B are trained; both copies stay frozen. Training minimises the KL divergence between the reader’s and the guesser’s answer distributions, over thirteen attributes of the user, from income to intent.

The bottleneck activation vuser is the user vector: the model’s picture of this user, compressed to what it needs to answer about them. Because it is learned through the intervention itself, the vector both encodes the model’s belief and, when written back, sets it.

Nothing in this recipe is specific to users. Any belief that can be put as a question about the situation the model thinks it is in and about the world, can be distilled using BSD.

Read, then Rewrite

BSD leaves us with a read-write interface for the extracted user vectors. With A, we read the model’s beliefs about a user, using one linear probe per attribute. With B, we change one of these beliefs by activation steering and observe the model’s response while the conversation and the question stay fixed.

Reading shows what the user vector encodes; writing tests whether the model uses it. Below you can find qualitative examples as well as evaluation results.

i. Reading

Once trained, the read half works on its own: the frozen model reads a new conversation, A projects its hidden state to vuser, and one linear probe per attribute decodes the belief.

Three real conversations, read by our monitor. The highlighted words are those it ties to the marked belief (found via gradient attribution).

ii. How well it reads

Measured on the test set, linear probes on vuser reach macro-F1 0.66–0.68 across all thirteen attributes; the same probes on random vectors reach 0.32.[11]

The 128-dimensional bottleneck keeps almost everything a linear readout finds in the full hidden state.

iii. Writing

To write a belief, we average the user vectors of training conversations for each value of an attribute. The difference between two such means, from the value the model holds to the one we want, is a direction in the user space. We add it, scaled and mapped back through B, to the model’s hidden state.

The conversation and the question stay fixed, so a change in the answer can only come from the edited belief. In this way we steer the model’s responses with the user vectors alone.

iv. How well it writes

Again over the test set: all 86 attribute-value pairs, 50 conversations each. Steering through vuser flips the belief in 78% of cases for Llama-3.1-8B, 51% for Qwen3-8B and 44% for OLMo-3-7B; random directions of the same norm flip 13%, 27% and 15%.[11]

Furthermore, we replace B with a random orthonormal projector, keeping the user vector and the steering direction the same. The learned write map steers substantially more strongly than the random one, winning in most pairs for all three models. The write map thus learns to write into directions of the residual stream to which the model is particularly responsive.

Same Request, Different User

We also want to know whether our distilled user vector affects safety-related behaviour. We take refusal as an example of such behaviour, since earlier work suggests that there might be a connection between the user profile and refusal.[8, 4]

We use StrongREJECT,[14] a benchmark of harmful requests, with Llama-3.1-8B. For each request, we steer the model along the user-intent direction, from adversarial to benign, and measure how often it still refuses. As a baseline, we steer along random directions of the same norm; as a control, we steer along an unrelated attribute, gender, from female to male.

Refusal under steering

Steering intent from adversarial to benign lowers refusal from 98% to 62%, although the request stays the same. Steering gender or a random direction lowers it only to 87%, so the effect is specific to the belief about intent rather than a result of changing the activations.[11]

Refusal falls but does not disappear. The belief about the user’s intent therefore modulates the safety decision rather than determining it: the model conditions refusal both on the request and on whom it believes it is talking to.

This has implications for safety. The model’s inferred user intent causally affects refusal. Safety evaluations that consider only the request and the response therefore miss part of what determines the model’s behaviour: its latent beliefs about the user.

Geometry of User Models

We first measure the anisotropy of the user space,[27] to see how information about users is distributed in it. Decomposing each user vector into its component along the centroid and the orthogonal residual, we find that the centroid carries about 80% of the energy; only 20% is user-specific.

Decoded through the probes, this centroid is a default user, on which the three models largely agree: male, European, middle-income, apolitical and benign. This default is likely specific to the corpus; but since WildChat consists of real conversations, it also approximates the model’s prior over users in ordinary conversations.

The models also agree on the residual that distinguishes one user from another: CKA[28] is 0.75 on average, against 0.01 for a permutation null. This shared geometry is functional: via Procrustes, we can find an orthogonal map with a global scale between the class centroids of two models and use it to transfer beliefs from one model to the other. On the disagreement set, the target model adopts the source’s belief in 53–54% of cases, against 30% for a random rotation.

Concluding Thoughts

We started with a question: what does a language model believe about its user? We treat a belief through its effect on the model’s behaviour, and therefore look for an internal state that is formed from the conversation and, when edited, changes the model’s responses. Belief Self-Distillation learns such a state as a compact user vector: it can be read with linear probes and written back to change the model’s answers. We also validated the user vector on a safety-related behaviour: changing the belief about the user’s intent modulates refusal. Across three independently trained models, this state also has a largely shared geometry.

Two points seem to us worth keeping beyond the method.

  • The limits of synthetic cues. What a model believes about its users should be studied on real conversations: probes trained on explicit cues barely transfer to unlabeled real conversations.
  • An illusion of alignment. Alignment often aims to suppress stereotyped statements about users. The explicit statements may be suppressed, but the stereotyped beliefs themselves may persist and keep shaping the model’s behaviour, creating an illusion of alignment.

Our approach also has limitations. BSD distils only the beliefs we ask about: thirteen predefined attributes, elicited through multiple-choice questions. The user vector is therefore shaped by this choice. We also study linear maps at a single layer, in three instruction-tuned models of similar size; whether the results hold across model sizes, layers and longer interactions remains open.

Follow-Up Directions

  1. Beyond multiple choice. Distil beliefs from other task formats, such as ranking or open-ended questions.
  2. More beliefs. Apply BSD to a wider range of beliefs, including ones not defined in advance.
  3. Other ways of writing. Try steering methods beyond activation addition, both in training and at inference.
